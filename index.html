What Swara does

Read

Textbooks, letters, bills and labels read aloud exactly as printed, with the original text always available.

Ask

Ask “When is it due?” and get a spoken answer drawn from the page, plus summaries of pages or chapters.

Find

On the phone: say “find my bottle”, sweep the room, and follow beeps and spoken directions.

Translate

Optional, announced Sinhala–English translation. The original text is never hidden.

Honest status: Swara is a student project (group CS-01, IIT / University of Westminster) in active development. Everything on this site describes what we are building and testing, not finished or clinically validated features.

How Swara works

Every step is designed so a person who cannot see the screen, the page edge or the camera can still succeed on the first try.

The station, step by step

  1. Slide the page into the corner. A plate with a corner stop means the page lands in the same place every time. Touch is the guide, not sight.
  2. Press the big button. One large, convex, tactile start button. A separate, smaller button handles shutdown so the two are never confused.
  3. The camera checks the page. An overhead autofocus camera under a steady LED light checks blur, glare and cropping, and speaks short hints such as “too dark”.
  4. Swara reads it aloud. The text is recognised, put into reading order and spoken in Sinhala or English.
  5. Turn the page, reading continues. Page-turn detection continues the chapter without pressing anything again.
  6. Ask or summarise by voice. “Repeat that”, “slower”, “summarise this page”. No touching needed.

Behind the scenes

Camera image → page detection and perspective correction → lighting and blur checks → script detection and OCR → reading order → original text → optional translation → speech → source-grounded questions.

Exact text first

We keep the raw OCR output next to any corrected text, especially for dates, bills and medicine labels.

Answers tied to the page

Questions are answered from the scanned text, and Swara should say so when it is unsure rather than guess.

Permission first

Swara asks before sending personal pages to cloud services, and exports are limited to schools or authorised organisations.

Three products, one voice

Choose the form that fits your day. All three share the same reading core, voice assistant and settings.

Swara Station

Standalone

A box on your table. Corner-stop plate, overhead gooseneck or clamp camera (target 12 MP autofocus), ring LED, speaker and microphone, with a Raspberry Pi inside that boots straight into the reader. No laptop or phone needed.

Swara Laptop

Plate + laptop

The same plate and camera plugged into a computer. Large high-contrast text follows the spoken sentence, with chapter reading, page-turn detection, chapter summaries and keyboard or voice control.

Swara Phone

Android first

For homes, shops and on the move. Three screens: Read (guided framing and automatic capture), Ask (voice questions) and Find (on-device object finding, no internet needed).

Why camera quality matters

On an A4 page, a 1080p webcam gives only about 92 pixels per inch, which is too soft for small print. A 4K camera gives about 185 and a 12 MP camera about 260. That’s why the station targets a 4K or 12 MP autofocus camera.

Design commitments

Contrast

We target WCAG 2.2 AA at minimum: 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text and controls.

Never colour alone

Status and state are always shown in words, shape or sound as well as colour.

Resizable text

A+ and A− buttons here; the product targets enlargement to 200% with nothing lost.

Big, labelled controls

Touch targets of about 44–48 px, text plus icon on every button, and bold focus outlines.

Screen-reader friendly

Proper headings, labels, roles and focus order for TalkBack, NVDA and other screen readers.

Feedback that isn’t noisy

Short spoken hints, optional earcons and vibration. Every error says what happened and how to fix it.

Physical accessibility

  • A large, convex start button, physically separate from the small shutdown button.
  • A corner stop so placement is learned by touch.
  • Fixed lighting that doesn’t depend on the room.
  • Voice equivalents for Back, Repeat, Pause, Slower and Faster.

Everyday use

At school

Slide in a textbook page, listen, ask “summarise this page”, then turn to the next. Teachers can export text or audio to authorised school systems.

At home

Read a bill or letter aloud and ask “How much is due?” Medicine labels are read exactly as printed, and Swara signals uncertainty rather than inventing missing dosage text.

On the move

Use the phone app to frame a page with spoken hints, or to find your bottle, cup or door with beeps and directions.

How we will test

Page capture

10 A4 pages per participant against popular phone apps, with randomised order.

OCR accuracy

At least 30 real images per language, scored with character and word error rates.

Answer reliability

30 answerable and 15 unanswerable questions, to catch made-up answers.

Accessible controls

Blind and low-vision users start, pause, repeat and recover without visual help.

Known limits

  • No Braille display output in the first version, and no tactile graphics.
  • Handwriting is out of scope.
  • Several core operations currently use cloud services, so connectivity and privacy trade-offs apply.
  • Sri Lankan statistics: a 2014–15 national survey found 1.7% blindness among adults aged 40+. It is not a figure for all Sri Lankans or for today.
We never use blindfolded sighted testers as a substitute for blind participants.

Sinhala and English, tested properly

A language listed on a product website doesn’t mean it reads well. We treat every language skill as its own test.

Five separate checks

1. Reading (OCR)

Sinhala fonts, diacritics, numerals and mixed Sinhala-English lines.

2. Speaking (TTS)

Is the voice clear, natural and comfortable over long reading?

3. Listening

Voice commands in Sinhala and English, including accents and noisy rooms.

4. Translation

Optional, announced aloud, with the original text always kept.

5. Interface language

Menus, hints and error messages in the language you choose.

How we will measure

  • At least 30 real photographed pages per language: textbooks, letters, bills and medicine packaging.
  • Ground truth typed by people, then scored with character error rate and word error rate, with English, Sinhala and mixed scores kept separate.
  • Blind listeners rate the voice for comprehension and comfort.
  • We test odd cases on purpose: small print, columns, poor light, currency and dates.
Earlier Sri Lankan research reports Sinhala Tesseract word accuracy improving from 53% to 86% in its own tests. That is encouraging, but it is not a result we can promise for Swara until we measure it ourselves.

Privacy, safety and ethics

You will be reading bills, letters and medicine labels. These are personal, and some are safety-critical.

Ask before upload

Swara asks permission before sending a personal page to a cloud service, and says clearly when it is working offline.

Controlled export

Saved text or audio can only be exported to a school or authorised organisation.

Read exactly as printed

For medicine labels and bills, Swara reads what is printed, flags uncertainty and never invents missing dosage text.

Original always available

Raw OCR output is kept next to any corrected, translated or summarised version.

Research ethics

  • Testing with people starts only after institutional ethics approval.
  • Consent is accessible, in Sinhala and English, and minors need parental consent.
  • Photographed documents are handled carefully and not kept longer than needed.
  • We report participants by what they need, not by assumptions about age.
  • We never replace blind participants with blindfolded sighted testers.
Swara is not a medical device. Always confirm medicine doses with a pharmacist or doctor.

Roadmap

We are building a working core in about nine weeks, then extending it through the year. We build in software first and order hardware once the design is proven.

  1. Week 1: Plan. Interview protocol, ethics request, competitor checklist and supplier contact sheet.
  2. Weeks 1–2: Benchmark. 30 pages per language for OCR, theme prototypes and Sinhala voice listening tests.
  3. Weeks 3–4: Capture. Webcam capture pipeline, with 50 controlled attempts to measure first-try framing and delay.
  4. Weeks 5–6: Voice. Press-to-talk workflow, an accessible laptop prototype and the phone’s Read, Ask and Find skeleton.
  5. Weeks 7–8: Integrate. Backend, page-turn detection and phone object-finding tests.
  6. Week 9: Review. Evidence table, demo, limitations and budget estimates.

After the core

  • Two rounds of usability testing with at least five blind or low-vision users each.
  • Cost comparison of the complete station, including power, housing and running costs.
  • Possible future: Braille display support and export.

Dates are plans and may change.

Frequently asked questions

Is Swara available to buy?

No. It is a student prototype in development. You can register your interest on the Get involved page.

Are you the first to read pages aloud or find objects?

No. Several good apps and devices already read documents, answer questions and find objects. We are testing whether a fixed-page station, local Sinhala and English quality, low cost and one shared workflow can serve some people better.

Do I need a phone or laptop for the station?

No. The station works standalone with its own speaker and microphone. The laptop and phone versions share the same reading core.

Does it work without internet?

Not fully yet. Several core steps currently use cloud services. Object finding on the phone is designed to work on the device, and we will test and report offline behaviour clearly.

Will it read handwriting?

No. Handwriting is outside the scope of this project.

Can it replace Braille?

No. Braille is a literacy medium in its own right. The first version is speech only. Braille display support is a possible future step, and we will ask Braille users what they need.

Can I change the colours and text size?

Yes. This website has a theme button and text size buttons, and the product will offer dark, light and custom themes with enlargement up to 200%.

How accurate is it?

We don’t know yet, and we won’t guess. Accuracy for Sinhala and English will be measured on real local pages and reported openly.

Who is building it?

Group CS-01, first-year BSc Computer Science students at IIT / University of Westminster, under academic supervision.
